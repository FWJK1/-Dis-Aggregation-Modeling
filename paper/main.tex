\begin{abstract}
Aggregation and fragmentation among polymers has long been an area of active research and utility in physical chemistry. 
Drawing inspiration from the dynamics of social formulation, we make a preliminary effort at minimizing cost and probability of domination among various potential fragmentation kernels. 
This work has relevance for any work formalizing the formulation, dissolving, and  regulation of groups of abstract individuals. 
Social groups are of particular interest, from musical subcultures to the regulation of corporate consolidation. 
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Things fall apart, Yeats wrote \cite{zotero-item-4381}.
Of course, they also come together.
What balance is struck between the two, and crucially, \textbf{what does this balance cost}?

To attempt an answer to this question, we turn to the physics of aggregation and fragmentation, the competing processes by which  particles combine to form larger clusters and break up into smaller ones.
As thoroughly explicated in \textit{A Kinetic View of Statistical Physics}, aggregation and fragmentation can be thought of as an infinite set of differential equations over all cluster sizes from one to infinity\cite{krapivskyKineticViewStatistical2010}.
Rates of change are based on current amounts of clusters and a kernel specifying reaction rates between clusters of various sizes.
Clusters of size one are termed monomers, and the formulation as a whole is termed the Smoluchowski equations.

While Krapivsky \textit{et al} term such differential equations master equations, their equations make a mean-field assumption:  they track the  \textit{expected} cluster size distribution and assume it deterministic rather than keeping track of the full spread of probability over the stochastic evolution of the system.
In other words, they track the \textit{count} of the cluster sizes, rather than the probability of \textit{each count} of each cluster.

A more complete accounting is given by the Marcus--Lushnikov process, in which $ ML^{(n)}(x, t) $ is a random number whose probability evolves according to a Markov Chain involving all other $ ML^{(n_i)}(x, t)  $  \cite{aldousDeterministicStochasticModels1999}.
These models typically rescale the total mass of the system to be $ 1 $, and are identical to the Smoluchowski equations if the kernel is homogenous, or as $ N $, the number of initial unitary particles considered, goes to infinity.
However, these models are far less tractable, and even in 2018 computing the time-evolving probability with only $ 48 $ initial monomers took around $ 3000 $ minutes\cite{manuchehrfarEvolutionCoagulationFragmentationStochastic2019}.

Regardless of the form, prior work has largely focused on either the time-evolution  of cluster sizes or the static steady state distribution of them.
Instead, this work focus on the interplay between the steady state rate of fragmentation, or the \textit{cost} of maintaining the steady state, and the distribution of cluster sizes.
More simply, using the Smoluchowski equation form, this work charts the pareto front between minimizing the steady state probability of having an oversized \textit{dominant} cluster and minimizing the number of units of mass continually split by fragmentation.

\subsection*{The problem}

We model competing aggregation and fragmentation over a variety of rate kernels in order to study the steady-state distribution of cluster sizes as well as the steady-state rate of fragmentation measured in units of mass removed from larger clusters.
Our question is simple: given a pattern of aggregation, what fragmentation technique minimize the proportion of dominant clusters and steady-state fragmentation rate?
Does this dual-minimization create a pareto front?

\subsection*{The model}

\subsubsection*{Aggregation}

Aggregation is generally the transition
$$
    c_{i} + c_{j} \xrightarrow[]{K_{ij}} c_{i+j}
$$
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $ N $ is the total amount of mass divided by the size of the smallest cluster, which we normalize to $ 1 $.
    \item $c$ is a cluster
    \item $i$ and $j$ are masses
    \item $K_{ij}$ is the rate of aggregation between clusters of mass $i$ and mass $j$ into a clusters of mass $i +j$
    \item $K$, then, is an finite symmetric matrix $K_{i,j}=K_{j, i}$ of dimensions $ N \times N $
\end{itemize}

With the ordinary differential equation for all cluster sizes $ c_k $ :
\begin{align}
    \frac{\mathrm{d} c_{k}}{\mathrm{d} t } = \dot{c}_k & = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i+j=k} K_{ij} c_{i} c_{j} - c_{k} \sum_{i \geq  1} K_{ik}c_{i} \label{eq:ag_main}
    \\       & = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i, j \leq  1} K_{ij} c_{i} c_{j} \Big[\delta_{i+j, k} - \delta_{i, k} - \delta_{j, k} \Big]
\end{align}
where $\delta$ is the Kronecker delta.

\subsubsection*{Fragmentation}

Fragmentation is generally the transition
$$
    c_{i+j}  \xrightarrow[]{F_{ij}} c_{i} + c_{j}
$$
where $F$ is an finite symmetric matrix of size $ N \times N  $ and $F_{ij}$ is the rate of fragmentation of a cluster of size $i+j$ into a cluster of size $i$ and another of size $j$.

The ordinary differential equation for fragmentation across all clusters is then::
\begin{align}
    \frac{\mathrm{d} c_{k}}{\mathrm{d} t } = \sum _{j \geq  1} F_{kj}c_{j+k} - \frac{1}{2} \sum _{i+j=k}F_{ij} \label{eq:frg_main}
\end{align}


\subsubsection*{Aggregation and Fragmentation}


Putting equations \eqref{eq:ag_main} and \eqref{eq:frg_main} together yields the overall differential equation:
\begin{align}
    \frac{\mathrm{d} c_{k}}{\mathrm{d} t }  = \frac{1}{2} \sum _{i+j=k} K_{ij}c_{i}c_{j} - c_{k}\sum _{j \geq  1}K_{kj}c_{j} + \sum _{j\geq  1} F_{kj} c_{j+k} - \frac{1}{2} c_{k} \sum _{i+j=k}F_{ij}
\end{align}
